# What I Value in a Banknote

A banknote should not merely display value. It should feel valuable.

That distinction explains much of my reaction to the proposed euro designs. I am not opposed to new themes, contemporary illustration, portraits, birds, stronger European symbolism, or even radical composition. What matters to me is whether the result still possesses the visual authority of money.

A banknote is not an advertisement, an app screen, a museum poster, or a corporate annual report. It belongs to a rare class of objects that must operate simultaneously as a practical tool, a security device, a cultural symbol, and a physical expression of institutional trust.

Its design should reflect all four roles.

## Detail as a signal of value

Traditional banknotes use dense networks of engraving, hatching, guilloches, fine typography, repeated patterns, layered imagery, microtext, borders, seals, numbering, and security features.

Some of this complexity has a direct technical purpose. It makes reproduction difficult and helps users verify authenticity. But it also performs a cultural function.

Visual density tells us that the object was difficult to produce.

It suggests care, control, permanence, and institutional capacity. Even when we do not consciously inspect every line, the accumulation of detail creates an impression of depth. The note seems to contain more information than can be absorbed in a single glance.

That matters because money is based on confidence. A banknote is, physically, a printed piece of material. Its value comes from the system behind it. The design helps make that invisible system perceptible.

The complexity is therefore not decorative excess. It is part of the note’s rhetoric of credibility.

## A good banknote works at two distances

The best banknotes have at least two levels of reading.

From a distance, the denomination should be immediately clear. The color, number, silhouette, and overall composition should distinguish one note from another.

Up close, however, the note should reveal another world: fine lines, miniature symbols, textures, transitions, hidden structures, and details that were not obvious before.

A good note is legible in one second but not exhausted in one second.

This is where many contemporary proposals feel insufficient to me. They work well as thumbnails on a website. They have clear subjects, polished colors, and immediate visual impact. Yet after the first impression, there may be little left to discover.

The image says: here is a bird, here is a portrait, here is a river.

Then it stops.

A banknote will be handled thousands of times and may circulate for decades. It should reward repeated contact. It needs visual reserves.

## Complexity is not the same as clutter

I am not arguing that every available space should be filled.

Complexity can be ordered. It can have rhythm, hierarchy, restraint, and areas of rest. The current euro series demonstrates this well: its architecture, lines, textures, lettering, holographic areas, and tonal fields form a dense composition without becoming unreadable.

The issue is not maximalism versus minimalism.

The issue is whether the design has enough internal structure to feel like a monetary object rather than an illustration placed on a colored rectangle.

Proposal E stands out to me because it preserves that internal structure. Its imagery is experimental, but the note still seems constructed from layers. The portraits do not simply sit on top of the design. They participate in a wider graphic system of lines, symbols, textures, circles, and transitions.

It is conceptually modern while still speaking the visual language of currency.

Proposal H comes closer than many others, although less fully. It retains some density and material presence. Several other designs feel smoother, flatter, and more complete at first glance, but also more finite.

## Why excessive cleanliness can feel artificial

A recurring feature of contemporary visual culture is frictionless perfection: immaculate gradients, saturated colors, smooth contours, evenly distributed elements, and surfaces without visible resistance.

This style can be attractive. It also resembles the imagery used in digital branding, software interfaces, public-relations campaigns, and machine-generated illustration.

The problem is not that such images are artificial. All graphic design is constructed.

The problem is that this visual language is associated with speed, scalability, and disposability. It is designed to communicate instantly across screens and platforms. A banknote has almost the opposite temporal role. It is physical, repeatedly handled, expected to age, and meant to represent continuity.

When a note looks too clean, too saturated, or too perfectly resolved, it can lose material presence. It begins to feel less printed and more rendered.

The result may be visually pleasant while still lacking monetary gravity.

## The design should respect the object’s use

A banknote also has a physical orientation and a social ritual.

It is counted, stacked, passed across counters, stored in wallets, inspected under light, and read quickly in horizontal sequences. These gestures form part of what a banknote is.

That is why a vertically oriented design can be amusing and revealing. A vertical image alone might work. But when the denomination, lettering, and entire hierarchy are also vertical, the note seems to assume that it will be held like a phone.

This is not automatically wrong. Conventions can change.

But novelty should emerge from the nature of the object, not simply transfer habits from the dominant medium of the period. A banknote does not need to imitate a screen to appear contemporary. Its physical difference from a screen may be one of its greatest strengths.

## Symbolism matters, but embodiment matters too

I am sympathetic to the European Central Bank’s desire to strengthen a shared European identity.

In a world increasingly structured around large geopolitical blocs, common cultural symbols can have real value. Portraits of scientists, artists, composers, writers, and advocates for peace can give the euro a more human and memorable identity. Rivers and birds can represent ecological continuity across borders.

The choice of one figure does not have to imply the rejection of every alternative. Beethoven’s presence need not diminish Mozart. A banknote series can present examples rather than issue a final ranking of European culture.

But the symbol alone is not enough.

A portrait of Marie Curie does not automatically produce a compelling banknote. A colorful bird does not automatically communicate ecological depth. The subject must be transformed through the craft and structure of monetary design.

Otherwise, the note risks becoming a display surface for approved imagery rather than an integrated object.

## What I ultimately want

I want banknotes that feel designed for long attention rather than quick approval.

I want them to communicate value through craft, layering, structure, and material presence. I want immediate recognition from afar and discovery at close range. I want contemporary ideas without surrendering the visual language that distinguishes currency from branding.

I am not asking for nostalgia.

I am asking for continuity of function.

A successful new euro note should not imitate old money, but it should understand why old money looks the way it does. The density, engraving, borders, textures, and repeated patterns were not arbitrary historical decoration. They helped turn printed material into a credible bearer of value.

Modern security technology may no longer require every traditional device. Yet the psychological and cultural role of visual complexity remains.

The strongest future banknote would therefore combine three qualities:

clarity at first sight,

depth under examination,

and enough symbolic force to represent Europe without looking like a temporary campaign.

A banknote should not simply show something beautiful.

It should make value visible.
