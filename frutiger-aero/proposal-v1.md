## 1. Executive summary

Living Systems presents Europe through six systems that make collective life possible.

The title refers to more than biology. A living system is active, interdependent, maintained, and capable of adaptation. Water networks, libraries, railways, theatres, wetlands, laboratories, and public institutions all depend on flows of people, material, energy, information, care, and time. None operates alone.

The visual language draws from Frutiger Aero, a retrospective name for an early twenty-first-century design culture associated with clear skies, water, foliage, glass, rounded geometry, humanist typography, skeuomorphism, and an optimistic relation between technology, public life, and the natural world. That language is treated as a civic design vocabulary rather than as period nostalgia. Its value lies in its capacity to make invisible systems appear clean, accessible, connected, and worth maintaining.

The six denominations form a dependency tree rather than a scale of importance.

Infrastructure supports information because knowledge requires a stable physical substrate: electricity, water, buildings, public administration, schools, laboratories, archives, and communication networks.

Information supports mobility because purposeful movement depends on maps, schedules, signals, forecasts, coordination, and shared data. Mobility is broader than transport. It is the capacity of people and institutions to move, connect, adapt, and evolve.

Mobility supports interpretation because culture grows through contact among different places, languages, experiences, and points of view. Movement multiplies perspectives.

Interpretation supports environmental stewardship because people are more likely to care for places they understand as meaningful. Culture produces attachment to a street, river, landscape, community, and shared inheritance.

Environmental stewardship supports the future because a healthy future requires continued maintenance and renewal of environments : ecological, social, and institutional.

The progression is one possible account of how European values relate. It does not claim that each system has only one cause or one consequence. The purpose is to create a coherent series in which every denomination has a distinct role and every transition can be understood.

The recto and verso carry different parts of the argument.

The recto shows an ordinary encounter with the system at a human scale: a public fountain, an information commons, a railway platform, a stage, a restored river park, or an observatory facing an open horizon.

The verso reveals the larger network that makes the encounter possible: utility infrastructure, information exchange, an intermodal transport hub, a cultural forum, a managed watershed, or a research landscape directed toward what has not yet been decided.

The viewer should not need prior knowledge of Frutiger Aero to understand the scenes. At first sight, each note should communicate value, colour, principal system, and spatial identity. At close range, it should reveal service routes, data flows, wayfinding, translation, ecological monitoring, maintenance, and links to the next denomination.

The governing requirement is:

> Each note must be immediately usable as money and progressively readable as a system.

## 2. Theme, narrative, and motifs

### 2.1 Theme

The theme concerns the systems through which Europeans live together.

Infrastructure, information, mobility, interpretation, environment, and future are not isolated policy areas. They are mutually dependent civic capacities. Their interdependence is usually difficult to see because successful systems recede into everyday life. Clean water arrives. A train appears at the platform. A library keeps a record. A theatre offers a different viewpoint. A river is restored. Research continues before its results are known.

Money is an appropriate place for this theme because currency is itself a public system. A banknote circulates through trust, standards, maintenance, recognition, and shared acceptance. It is ordinary in use but institutional in meaning.

The shared European dimension lies in networks that cross borders without erasing local difference. Rail, research, culture, watersheds, public services, and environmental responsibility are experienced locally but depend on wider cooperation. The series therefore presents Europe through connection rather than through a catalogue of national emblems.

The central tension is optimism and responsibility.

The visual language is optimistic. It presents clean air, clear water, legible systems, accessible institutions, and technology integrated with public life. The proposal does not treat these conditions as automatic. Versoes reveal maintenance, coordination, repair, access, and stewardship. The optimism is credible because it is supported by work.

### 2.2 Narrative

The common story is functional and cumulative.

On the recto, the viewer encounters a system through a specific public experience.

On the verso, the system expands into a network of institutions, spaces, and flows.

Turning the note over changes scale. The first side is immediate and person-sized. The second side shows interdependence.

Across the series:

Infrastructure creates a reliable substrate.

Information allows that substrate to be understood and coordinated.

Mobility turns coordination into movement and adaptation.

Interpretation turns movement and difference into meaning.

Environment gives that meaning a field of responsibility.

Future extends responsibility beyond the present generation.

The €50 marks the main change of register. The first three denominations concern foundations, knowledge, and movement. The €50 asks what those capacities mean. It introduces culture not as entertainment but as interpretation, the activity through which a plural society understands itself.

The €200 does not close the sequence with a final answer. It closes it with an open question:

> What will shared systems allow Europe to become?

### 2.3 Motifs

€5, Infrastructure

The literal subject is the public-service substrate of everyday life: water, power, sanitation, maintenance, streets, civic planting, and accessible public space. Grey is interpreted through pale concrete, stainless steel, mist, glass, and printed graphite. The recto centres on a public water square and service pavilion. The verso expands into a civic utilities campus and the neighbourhoods it sustains.

€10, Information

The literal subject is the public creation, preservation, verification, and circulation of knowledge. It includes computer science, telecommunications, libraries, archives, research, and open civic information. Red becomes the colour of signal, routing, attention, and active connection rather than alarm. The recto centres on an information commons. The verso reveals a public knowledge and data network.

€20, Mobility

The literal subject is purposeful movement. Transport provides the visible channel through rail, tram, bicycle, walking routes, navigation, ports, and coordinated logistics. Blue corresponds to sky, distance, maps, and public transport systems. The recto centres on a train platform at the moment of departure. The verso expands into an intermodal station whose routes connect urban and regional landscapes.

€50, Interpretation

The literal subject is culture as an activity of making meaning. Theatre, museums, cinema, music, literature, design, conservation, translation, and public debate are different forms of interpretation. Orange-brown becomes stage amber, warm timber, terracotta, paper, and museum light. The recto centres on a performance space. The verso expands into a cultural forum that places creation, conservation, translation, and public participation around one shared civic interior.

€100, Environment

The literal subject is stewardship of the living world and the built environments within it. The note presents restoration, biodiversity, water systems, renewable energy, monitoring, resilient agriculture, and urban ecology. Green directly supports recognition. The recto centres on a restored river park. The verso reveals the watershed and the coordinated work required to sustain it.

€200, Future

The literal subject is the capacity to act beyond the present. It includes high education, Earth observation, space, long-term planning, ecological renewal, technologies such particle accelerators, nuclear fusion, whose final form is not yet known. Yellow-brown becomes dawn, solar light, warm glass, pale gold, and open sky. The recto centres on a research observatory facing the horizon. The verso presents a broad landscape of inquiry and unfinished possibility.

### 2.4 Why these motifs

The motif family is suitable for banknotes because it combines recognition with depth.

Each denomination has a clear public subject. A fountain, library, train, stage, river, or horizon can be identified quickly. Each subject also opens into a network suitable for close inspection.

The motifs support a common visual grammar of flows, nodes, transparent layers, public spaces, and managed landscapes. They can be differentiated through scene type, silhouette, movement, border rhythm, and colour.

The recto-verso relationship is consistent across all six notes without becoming repetitive. Each recto isolates an everyday encounter. Each verso reveals the system behind it.

The themes are European without depending on one nation. Their meaning arises from shared civic life, cross-border connection, and collective responsibility.

Each motif also provides simple forms suitable for later security development: droplets, signal nodes, route lines, stage apertures, leaf and river structures, and horizon arcs.

## 3. Assessment framework

Living Systems is structured against the published euro-banknote design framework.

The official process requires a coherent visual narrative across the €5, €10, €20, €50, €100, and €200 denominations; clear distinction between notes; accessibility; technical feasibility; common European elements; and consistency between theme and imagery. It also requires provision for the European flag, the map of Europe, a signature area, tactile identification, and later integration of security features.

The published visual and technical criteria are:

| Criterion                                           | Published weighting |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------: |
| Visual harmony and coherence of the complete series |                 10% |
| Thematic consistency with the story                 |                 10% |
| Visual appeal and aesthetic quality                 |                 20% |
| Symbolism and cultural representation of the EU     |                 10% |
| Discrimination between denominations                |                 10% |
| Public recognition                                  |                  5% |
| Accessibility                                       |                 10% |
| Creativity and originality                          |                 25% |

The official report identifies visual clarity, denomination distinction, accessibility, public relevance, and balance between creativity and practicality as central considerations.

The common system consists of:

a shared recto and verso grid;

a common humanist typographic system;

stable institutional fields;

a controlled relationship between pale paper, denomination colour, and dark structural line;

a shared viewpoint family;

a consistent hierarchy of large scene, supporting network, and fine detail.

The principal variables are:

denomination proportion;

dominant colour;

recto setting;

verso system;

movement pattern;

border geometry;

identifier content;

light-dark distribution;

tactile group.

European content is carried through public services, knowledge institutions, transport, culture, environmental cooperation, and intergenerational responsibility.

Accessibility is supported through dimensions, numerals, contrast, silhouette, border pattern, tactile reservations, and scene type, not colour alone.

Security features are not presented as finished technical solutions. Stable zones and transformable motifs are identified for later specialist development.

## 4. Conceptual basis

### 4.1 Frutiger Aero as a design language

Frutiger Aero is treated as a system of representation rather than as an applied surface style.

Five structural properties make it relevant.

First, it makes systems visible through flow.

Water, light, air, data, roads, and organic growth are often represented as clear paths rather than hidden mechanisms. This supports scenes about public utilities, communication, transport, culture, and ecology.

Second, it places technology inside ordinary life.

Glass structures, screens, public signs, transit systems, and clean infrastructure appear alongside trees, water, open sky, and people. Technology is not isolated as spectacle. It is represented as an accessible civic tool.

Third, it uses optimism spatially.

Open horizons, bright atmospheres, transparent materials, and generous public space create a sense that institutions can be legible and the future can remain open.

Fourth, it joins organic and geometric forms.

Leaves, droplets, bubbles, waves, grids, and rounded structures allow natural and civic systems to share one visual language.

Fifth, it supports immediate recognition.

Simple silhouettes and clear colour fields can coexist with layered diagrams, reflections, route patterns, and small public activities.

### 4.2 Comprehension without prior subject knowledge

A viewer without knowledge of the visual reference should be able to read the notes directly.

€5: public water, light, streets, sanitation, and maintenance sustain an ordinary civic square.

€10: a library and data network allow information to be created, preserved, and shared.

€20: a railway hub helps people and goods move with purpose.

€50: a theatre and cultural forum turn different experiences into shared meaning.

€100: a restored river landscape is cared for through monitoring, planning, and public participation.

€200: research and observation preserve the capacity to choose among futures not yet fixed.

Familiarity with the visual reference adds another layer. It reveals the purposeful use of glass, bubbles, clean skies, leaf-water imagery, humanist typography, rounded transport forms, and optimistic eco-technology.

That familiarity is supplementary. The banknotes remain intelligible without it.

### 4.3 European scope

Europe is represented through systems that are local in experience and continental in operation.

Public utilities are municipal but shaped by shared standards.

Information moves among schools, libraries, universities, laboratories, archives, media, and communication networks.

Mobility crosses regions and borders through interoperable routes.

Culture grows through translation, travel, preservation, and encounter.

Environmental systems cross political boundaries through rivers, air, migration, climate, and shared ecosystems.

The future depends on cooperation among generations and institutions.

The Europe map appears without internal national borders. It is treated as a field of routes, watersheds, signals, and shared observation. The silhouette, projection, orientation, and scale remain stable across the series.

Architectural references are plural. They draw from European civic modernism, railway halls, libraries, theatres, museums, public squares, research campuses, waterworks, and ecological parks without assigning one monument to represent the continent.

### 4.4 Principal subject

The real subject is collective capacity.

Buildings, vehicles, devices, landscapes, and people are evidence of systems at work. None is treated as an isolated icon.

Figures are participants, users, maintainers, performers, researchers, travellers, and observers. They provide scale and social meaning. No portrait dominates the series.

The environment carries equal weight. A station is understood through routes and movement. A theatre is understood through performers, audience, translation, and memory. A river is understood through ecological relations and stewardship.

### 4.5 Visual fidelity to the chosen language

Recognition depends on a controlled set of visual characteristics:

bright but not blank skies;

clear water and refracted light;

rounded glass and translucent surfaces;

humanist sans-serif typography;

white and pale neutral structures;

fresh greens and cyans used as accents;

clean public space;

organic motifs joined to geometric networks;

optimistic representations of technology;

soft atmospheric depth.

The main risks are drift into adjacent styles.

Generic corporate eco-branding would flatten the theme into leaves and slogans.

Science-fiction concept art would replace civic accessibility with spectacle.

Skeuomorphic interface imitation would make the note resemble obsolete software.

Generic stock-photo collage would weaken monetary structure.

Luxury gloss would undermine public character.

The degree of realism is idealised but credible. Buildings, transport systems, parks, and interiors should appear usable and maintainable. Perspective may be clarified and spatial relations compressed, but the world should not become physically impossible.

The permissible idealisation concerns cleanliness, legibility, light, and coordination. It does not permit floating architecture, decorative holograms without function, or a city without labour and maintenance.

The art direction is:

> Preserve the visual optimism and system clarity of the source language, but translate them into public space and monetary structure rather than imitating a screen.

## 5. Reference to Frutiger Aero and monetary precedent

The primary visual reference is Frutiger Aero.

The study takes from it:

the union of technology and nature;

clear skies and water;

rounded transparent forms;

humanist typography;

clean public infrastructure;

organic flow patterns;

a belief that complex systems can appear accessible;

an atmosphere of civic and technological optimism.

The study does not copy:

desktop wallpapers;

operating-system controls;

application icons;

advertising layouts;

product photography;

literal glass buttons;

floating bubbles used only as ornament;

recognisable software branding.

Monetary precedent provides a second, limited reference.

The euro design process demonstrates the value of a coherent series, denomination distinction, common institutional elements, and a narrative relationship between front and reverse. The official design proposals also show that immediate familiarity can coexist with secondary symbolic detail.

The transfer is methodological. The notes retain large numerals, stable fields, borders, map, flag, scripts, tactile reservations, and security-ready areas.

The transformation lies in the visual language. Traditional density is rebuilt through transparent overlays, route networks, water ripples, leaf structures, optical fibres, architectural detail, and layered public scenes.

## 6. Narrative structure

### 6.1 Recto role

The recto presents the system as it is encountered in ordinary life.

Every recto answers three questions:

What does this system feel like at human scale?

Where does a person meet it?

What immediate public value does it provide?

The recto must remain calm and legible. It must not become a policy diagram, a catalogue of institutions, or a miniature version of the verso.

The main scene is complete, but it leaves a reason to turn the note over: the visible service implies a larger network.

### 6.3 Verso role

The verso reveals the network behind the recto encounter.

Every verso has one centre of gravity:

€5: a civic utilities campus;

€10: a public information exchange;

€20: an intermodal railway hub;

€50: a cultural forum;

€100: a restored watershed;

€200: an open research landscape.

Every verso answers three questions:

What larger system makes the recto experience possible?

Who maintains, uses, or interprets it?

What wider public capacity does it create?

Supporting activities remain spatially subordinate. The reverse is one world, not an inventory.

Recto and verso depict related but not necessarily identical places. Conceptual continuity matters more than literal before-and-after continuity.

### 6.4 Series progression

€5 provides the substrate.

€10 makes the substrate legible and coordinated.

€20 turns coordination into purposeful movement.

€50 introduces a change of register: movement produces encounter, and encounter produces interpretation.

€100 extends interpretation into belonging and stewardship.

€200 extends stewardship into responsibility for conditions that do not yet have a fixed outcome.

The final denomination closes the sequence by opening it. It does not depict one predicted future. It depicts the capacity to remain able to choose.

## 7. Monetary design principles

### 7.1 Visual authority

The notes must read as money before the theme is analysed.

Each design integrates:

a large denomination numeral;

EURO, ΕΥΡΩ, and ЕВРО;

the European flag;

a stable Europe map;

a signature field;

serial areas;

tactile reservations;

security-ready zones;

an outer legal frame;

a middle theme-derived pattern;

a controlled tonal hierarchy.

The monetary scaffold is not placed over a finished illustration. Narrative image, numeral, institutional field, and border are composed together.

### 7.2 Detail as evidence of value

Visual density communicates care, continuity, and institutional capacity.

Detail is concentrated in:

utility routes;

pipes and channels;

fibre paths;

library structures;

station roofs and track systems;

wayfinding;

stage machinery;

museum displays;

translation devices;

river vegetation;

monitoring stations;

research instruments;

maps;

borders;

micro-patterns.

The density should imply that the note contains more information than can be absorbed at once.

### 7.3 Complexity without clutter

Three scales govern the composition.

Large forms establish denomination recognition: fountain, information hall, train, stage, river, horizon.

Medium-scale systems explain the narrative: service network, data exchange, station circulation, cultural forum, watershed, research landscape.

Fine detail rewards inspection: maintenance access, captions rendered as controlled microtext, route numbers, ecological species, archival objects, material patterns, and cross-denomination links.

Visual rest is preserved in sky, water, pale walls, glass, open floor, and river surface.

### 7.4 Two distances of reading

From a distance, the viewer reads:

denomination;

physical proportion;

colour family;

main silhouette;

overall light-dark distribution.

At close range, the viewer discovers:

maintenance and service routes;

information layers;

transport coordination;

translation and restoration;

ecological monitoring;

future-facing research;

links between denominations;

small public activities;

security-ready patterns.

The series must be tested as thumbnails, in grayscale, and under partial occlusion.

### 7.5 Print language

The notes combine monetary linework with controlled translucency.

Engraving and fine vector line define numerals, borders, maps, building structure, routes, and small details.

Stipple and halftone model sky, water, cloud, foliage, and glass.

Flat colour fields establish denomination identity.

Translucent overprints create water, refracted light, and system flow without making the surface resemble a backlit display.

Guilloche patterns derive from each system: conduits, signals, routes, interpretive waves, leaf-river branching, and horizon arcs.

Rendered imagery may be used as a source for spatial development, but final surfaces should be translated into controlled print structure.

No denomination should look like an advertisement, software screen, or poster.

### 7.6 Horizontal use

All principal elements are readable horizontally.

The large numeral remains visible when notes are stacked or fanned.

Secondary numerals appear near opposite corners.

Scenes move laterally, supporting quick counting and repeated inspection.

Contemporary character comes from the concept, colour, and treatment of public systems, not from copying a display ratio or interface layout.

## 8. Colour policy

### 8.1 General rule

The series is luminous, but not neon.

Freshness comes from high lightness, clean paper, transparent overlays, and local chromatic accents rather than full-surface saturation.

Colour should appear layered into paper and translucent ink. Glass, water, and sky may carry smooth tonal transitions, but large areas should retain visible print texture.

Strong colour is restricted to signal paths, route markers, stage light, foliage, ecological indicators, and future-facing light.

### 8.2 Shared palette structure

Each denomination uses:

a near-white paper tone;

a clear but controlled denomination hue;

a dark structural tone;

a limited high-chroma accent.

Transitions are produced through translucent overprint, halftone, line density, and pale atmospheric gradients.

The denomination hue should occupy enough area for recognition but should not suppress the paper.

### 8.4 Theme-specific colour risk

The €10 carries the greatest risk.

Red can imply warning, commercial urgency, or danger. The note therefore uses red as signal and circulation. Large fields remain pale rose, white, and clear neutral. Strong red appears in fibre routes, indexing marks, data nodes, and the principal numeral.

The €50 also requires control. Orange-brown can become nostalgic or heavy. The solution is to use warm translucent amber, pale paper, and terracotta rather than dark sepia.

The €200 must avoid luxury coding. Gold appears as dawn and stored light, not as metallic wealth.

### 8.5 Grayscale performance

The notes remain distinguishable through:

physical proportion;

large numerals;

recto silhouettes;

verso spatial types;

border rhythms;

tactile groups;

density;

light-dark balance.

The €5 is low and horizontal.

The €10 is branched and nodal.

The €20 is directional and linear.

The €50 is concentric and theatrical.

The €100 is branching and landscape-based.

The €200 is open and horizon-led.

The image ends at the note edge. Presentation margins, background surfaces, rotation, perspective skew, and drop shadows are excluded from the design plate.

### 9.2 Shared recto composition

The recto uses four overlapping fields.

The left value field occupies approximately 18 percent of width. It contains the principal numeral, secondary numeral, tactile reservation, and denomination pattern.

The principal image field occupies approximately 53 percent. It contains the everyday system encounter.

The institutional field occupies approximately 26 percent. It contains the flag, Europe map, euro scripts, signature field, serial reservation, and secondary numeral.

The fields overlap through light, pattern, and transparent structure. They should not read as separate panels.

### 9.3 Shared verso composition

The left value field occupies approximately 15 percent.

The principal narrative field occupies approximately 68 percent and contains one dominant system.

The right institutional field occupies approximately 17 percent and contains map, serial areas, stars, secondary numeral, and reserved technical space.

A denomination-specific flow crosses the principal field:

utility channel;

information route;

transport line;

interpretive wave;

river branch;

horizon arc.

The central narrative remains dominant.

### 9.9 Numerals and lettering

The typography uses the Frutiger typeface.

It draws on the openness, legibility, and rounded terminals associated with early twenty-first-century public information design.

The principal numeral is broad, calm, and immediately legible. It uses generous counters, moderate stroke contrast, and subtle line engraving.

EURO, ΕΥΡΩ, and ЕВРО share a stable horizontal hierarchy.

Small technical text uses a companion humanist grotesk or monospaced face for routes, coordinates, and microtext.

### 9.10 Institutional integration

The flag, scripts, signature field, and map share the note's paper tone and line structure.

The Europe map uses one stable silhouette and projection. Each denomination changes its internal treatment:

€5 service grid;

€10 signal network;

€20 route field;

€50 cultural connections;

€100 watershed and habitat lines;

€200 observation arcs.

The official elements remain recognisable and are not dissolved into decoration.

### 9.11 The twelve stars

The official twelve-star symbol remains accurate in number, arrangement, and orientation.

Secondary star-like structures may appear in data constellations, stage lights, ecological indicators, or horizon points, but they do not compete with the flag.

## 10. Visual environment and human presence

### 11.1 Visual environment

The series combines public modernism, ecological design, and early twenty-first-century civic optimism.

Common materials include:

glass;

white or pale concrete;

stainless steel;

light timber;

ceramic tile;

clear water;

green roofs;

translucent membranes;

printed wayfinding;

soft landscape surfaces.

Architecture uses broad canopies, clear entrances, rounded corners, generous daylight, legible circulation, and visible public access.

Avoid ornamental futurism, private luxury architecture, corporate headquarters imagery, and sterile spaces without users.

### 11.2 Terrain, space, and perspective

Rectos use one coherent human-scale view with moderate depth.

Versos use elevated oblique panoramas or wide controlled interiors.

Sectional transparency may reveal pipes, routes, archives, or ecological layers, but the view must remain spatially coherent.

Multiple scales may be compressed through overlays, not through incompatible perspective.

### 11.3 Human figures

Figures participate.

They drink, read, maintain, travel, perform, listen, restore, monitor, teach, study, and observe.

They remain small enough that the system is the subject.

Crowds are diverse in age, clothing, mobility, and activity. No group becomes an emblematic national type.

### 11.4 Cultural plurality

Plurality appears through:

different languages in controlled microtext;

varied public-space habits;

multiple transport modes;

translation and captioning;

different artistic forms;

urban and rural landscapes;

contrasting architectural periods;

shared participation rather than token portraiture.

## 26. Evaluation summary

### 27.1 Visual harmony and coherence

All six notes share a horizontal grid, recto encounter, verso network, humanist typography, stable institutional fields, controlled luminous palette, viewpoint family, and three-scale density hierarchy.

Coherence does not require identical scenes. Family resemblance comes from composition and treatment while each denomination retains a different spatial identity.

### 27.2 Thematic consistency

Every denomination describes a system.

The recto shows how it is encountered.

The verso shows what sustains it and what it enables.

The six roles form one functional sequence from infrastructure to open future.

### 27.3 Visual appeal and aesthetic quality

Large silhouettes provide clarity.

Medium-scale civic scenes provide narrative.

Fine routes, surfaces, activities, and patterns provide depth.

High-lightness fields and restrained translucency create freshness without turning the notes into screens.

Quiet sky, water, glass, wall, and floor areas preserve hierarchy.

### 27.4 Symbolism and cultural representation of the EU

The series represents:

public services;

shared knowledge;

cross-border mobility;

translation and cultural participation;

environmental stewardship;

research and intergenerational responsibility.

Europe is presented through interdependence rather than national ranking.

### 27.5 Denomination discrimination

Independent signals include:

physical proportion;

colour family;

principal recto silhouette;

dominant verso subject;

border pattern;

tonal distribution;

tactile group;

movement structure.

### 27.6 Public recognition

The direct scenes remain understandable without specialist knowledge.

Fountain, library, train, stage, river, and horizon provide immediate anchors.

### 27.7 Accessibility

The proposal provides large numerals, strong local contrast, stable tactile reservations, denomination-specific patterns, distinct silhouettes, grayscale structure, and multiple recognition cues.

Final accessibility performance requires specialist testing.

### 27.8 Creativity and originality

The original argument lies in treating a visual language often associated with consumer technology as a disciplined language for public systems.

The aesthetic is not applied as decoration. Its flows, transparency, organic geometry, and optimism are used to explain interdependence across both sides and all six denominations.

## 27. Closing statement

Living Systems begins with the public substrate of ordinary life and ends with a horizon that remains open.

On every recto, a person meets a system.

On every verso, the larger network becomes visible.

Infrastructure supports information.

Information supports mobility.

Mobility supports interpretation.

Interpretation creates belonging.

Belonging supports stewardship.

Stewardship preserves the future as possibility.

The series presents Europe as a living arrangement of services, knowledge, movement, culture, environment, and responsibility.

A banknote belongs to the same class of object. It is printed matter made public through trust, circulation, maintenance, and common recognition.
