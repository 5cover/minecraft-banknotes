## 1. Executive summary

The executive summary should explain the complete concept without requiring prior knowledge of the theme, its visual references, or any specialised source material.

Begin by defining the subject in plain language. When the theme draws from a game, artistic movement, scientific field, historical period, social practice, or technical system, describe only the features needed to understand the banknotes. Do not assume that a juror recognises the source material.

Explain why the theme is appropriate for a euro banknote series. The connection should arise from the theme's ability to represent shared European experience, institutions, values, environments, achievements, or responsibilities. Avoid arguments based only on popularity, novelty, nostalgia, or visual appeal.

State the common relationship between recto and verso. This relationship may concern origin and consequence, individual and collective activity, environment and institution, observation and interpretation, source and circulation, or another project-specific pairing. The two sides should form one argument rather than two unrelated illustrations.

Explain how the six denominations form a sequence. The sequence does not need to represent increasing historical importance, monetary worth, scale, or chronology, but it must have an intelligible ordering. Constraints such as prescribed denomination colours may influence the assignments. The proposal should acknowledge those constraints and explain how a coherent narrative is built within them.

State how the series avoids six interchangeable notes. Name the dominant subject, spatial type, or compositional gesture assigned to each denomination. Supporting details may be extensive, but every note should have one large-scale identity that survives reduction to a thumbnail or grayscale silhouette.

Explain the two levels of reading.

At first sight, the viewer should recognise value, colour family, principal motif, and overall composition.

At close range, the note should reveal symbolic relationships, secondary scenes, material or historical references, recurring patterns, institutional elements, and links to the other denominations.

End the summary with one governing requirement. It should be brief enough to guide later decisions. A suitable formulation addresses immediate recognition and sustained attention without presenting those goals as opposites.

## 2. Theme, narrative, and motifs

### 2.1 Theme

Define the theme as an argument about Europe rather than as a catalogue of attractive subjects.

Explain:

1. what the theme concerns;
2. why it belongs on money;
3. what shared European dimension it reveals;
4. what it allows the series to say that a conventional portrait, monument, landscape, or wildlife series would not say in the same way.

The theme should remain broader than any one denomination. It must support six distinct motifs without becoming so broad that almost any image could fit inside it.

Identify the central tension or contrast that gives the theme depth. Examples include permanence and change, public systems and private experience, natural continuity and human intervention, local specificity and European connection, optimism and responsibility, or memory and renewal.

### 2.2 Narrative

State the common story carried across the six notes.

Explain the function of the recto, the function of the verso, and the relationship created by turning the note over. The narrative should exploit the physical fact that a banknote has two sides.

Clarify whether the sequence is:

cumulative;

chronological;

geographical;

functional;

conceptual;

cyclical;

or organised by another principle.

When later denominations depend on earlier ones, explain that dependence. When the sequence contains a deliberate interruption or change of register, identify it and explain why it improves the whole series.

The narrative should be strong enough that the motifs would still make sense if described without colour.

### 2.3 Motifs

Describe each denomination motif in one concise paragraph.

For every motif, state:

its literal subject;

its thematic function;

its relation to the prescribed denomination colour;

its distinct contribution to the sequence;

its capacity to produce a clear recto and verso.

Do not treat the six motifs as independent commissions. Explain how they divide the theme into complementary parts.

Where two motifs were seriously considered for the same denomination, record the comparison only when it clarifies the final decision. The comparison should address colour, symbolism, public recognition, visual distinction, and series balance. It should not read as indecision.

### 2.4 Why these motifs

Explain why the chosen motif family is suitable for a banknote series.

A good motif family should support:

immediate recognition;

variation across six denominations;

a common visual grammar;

meaningful recto-verso relationships;

close-reading detail;

later transformation into security imagery;

a European interpretation that does not depend on one country.

Clarify what the motifs represent beyond their literal appearance. The proposal should show why they carry the theme rather than merely decorate it.

## 3. Assessment framework

The proposal should state that it is structured against the published euro-banknote design criteria.

Summarise the framework accurately. The official process requires a coherent visual narrative across the €5, €10, €20, €50, €100, and €200 denominations, clear distinction between notes, accessibility, technical feasibility, common European elements, and consistency between theme and imagery. It also requires provision for the European flag, the map of Europe, a signature area, tactile identification, and later integration of security features.

Include the published visual and technical criteria:

| Criterion | Published weighting |
| --------- | ------------------: |
| Visual harmony and coherence of the complete series | 10% |
| Thematic consistency with the story | 10% |
| Visual appeal and aesthetic quality | 20% |
| Symbolism and cultural representation of the EU | 10% |
| Discrimination between denominations | 10% |
| Public recognition | 5% |
| Accessibility | 10% |
| Creativity and originality | 25% |

Explain how the concept responds to these requirements without claiming success by assertion alone.

A strong framework section identifies:

the common system governing all six notes;

the variables that differentiate denominations;

the European content carried by the theme;

the role of accessibility beyond colour;

the areas reserved for later technical development;

the boundary between a concept design and a production-ready banknote.

Security features should not be presented as finished technical solutions unless specialist work has actually been completed. The proposal should instead identify images, zones, patterns, or transitions that could support later security development.

## 4. Conceptual basis

### 4.1 [Chosen subject or visual language] as a design language

Explain how the theme operates as a system of representation rather than as an applied image or decorative style.

Identify three to five structural properties that make it useful for a banknote series. Depending on the project, these may concern:

movement;

modularity;

ecological interdependence;

public service;

information flow;

material transformation;

cultural transmission;

shared rituals;

infrastructure;

scientific observation;

human cooperation;

or another recurring principle.

For each property, explain how it can produce visible relationships across the two sides of a note.

The section should establish that the theme is capable of carrying complex civic meaning. It should not rely on familiarity, fandom, trend value, or surface resemblance.

### 4.2 Comprehension without prior subject knowledge

Explain how each primary scene can be understood by a viewer who has never encountered the source material.

List the plain visual readings of the six denominations in one sentence each. These readings should describe visible actions or relationships rather than specialist references.

Then explain what additional knowledge contributes. Specialist familiarity may reveal mechanics, historical references, species behaviour, technical functions, artistic quotations, or symbolic correspondences, but it must not be required for basic comprehension.

The design should reward expertise without excluding non-specialists.

### 4.3 European scope

Explain how the theme represents Europe without assigning one country the role of representing the whole continent.

Possible strategies include:

shared institutions;

cross-border systems;

common environments;

interdependent infrastructure;

cultural practices found in several regions;

scientific and artistic traditions;

public spaces;

routes and networks;

ecological continuity;

plural architectural or visual references.

State how the Europe map is treated. Internal national borders should not become the primary organising device unless the theme specifically requires them.

Avoid collecting one emblem from each country. That approach often produces a checklist rather than a coherent visual system.

### 4.4 Principal subject

Define what the series treats as its real subject.

The principal subject may be:

collective systems;

individual contribution;

public spaces;

natural processes;

shared memory;

public services;

scientific inquiry;

cultural exchange;

or another project-specific focus.

Explain the role of people, portraits, animals, buildings, objects, or symbols in relation to that subject.

When figures are present, specify whether they are protagonists, participants, witnesses, users, or scale references. When no figure dominates, explain why the environment or system carries the meaning more effectively.

### 4.5 Visual fidelity to the chosen language

Define what must remain recognisable in the theme's source language and what may be interpreted through banknote design.

This section should be especially precise when the project draws from an established visual movement, a game, a historical medium, a scientific imaging system, or a well-known design culture.

Identify:

the canonical forms, proportions, textures, symbols, or spatial habits that establish recognition;

the generic substitutes that would cause drift into an adjacent style;

the degree of realism or abstraction;

the permitted level of idealisation;

the treatment of perspective, atmosphere, and surface;

the elements that must remain consistent across all denominations.

Do not ask the visual language to become "more creative" as a default. Define the target and the boundaries.

End with one short art-direction sentence that distinguishes preservation from imitation and interpretation from drift.

## 5. Reference to [reference design, movement, or precedent]

Use this section only when a prior banknote design, artistic work, design movement, or visual system provides a meaningful methodological reference.

Describe the reference accurately and identify the precise qualities being studied.

Possible qualities include:

density of detail;

balance between quiet and active fields;

recto-verso alignment;

treatment of a central motif;

integration of symbols;

typographic hierarchy;

colour restraint;

material presence;

series continuity;

or the relationship between immediate and close reading.

Then state what is not being copied.

Separate transferable principles from theme-specific composition. A good reference section should prevent contamination rather than encourage it.

Define how the new design transforms the reference. The transformation may change the subject, hierarchy, recurring device, spatial organisation, colour behaviour, or symbolic logic while retaining selected design lessons.

When several references are used, assign each one a limited role. One may guide density, another colour, another scene composition, and another typography. Do not combine them without boundaries.

## 6. Narrative structure

### 6.1 Recto role

Define the narrative and visual role of the recto.

The recto may establish origin, identity, observation, individual contribution, environment, present condition, or another first state.

State what the recto must answer. Use three project-specific questions.

For example:

Where is the subject encountered?

What condition or context defines it?

What should be understood before the note is turned over?

Also state what the recto must not become. Exclusions may concern excessive explanation, diagrams, crowded supporting scenes, literal timelines, spectacle, or premature presentation of the verso meaning.

The recto should be complete, but it should leave a conceptual reason to turn the note over.

### 6.2 Recurring identifier or visual anchor

Define the stable device, motif field, portrait zone, medallion, emblem, aperture, label, or other anchor repeated across all rectos.

If the series intentionally has no separate anchor, explain which fixed composition element performs the same recognition function.

Specify:

position;

relative scale;

shape;

contents;

background treatment;

relationship to the main scene;

relationship to later security development;

what remains identical;

what changes by denomination.

The anchor should perform a limited number of clear functions. Avoid loading it with every part of the narrative.

State what it does not contain. This is often as important as stating what it contains.

### 6.3 Verso role

Define the narrative and visual role of the verso.

The verso may show consequence, shared activity, public use, collective interpretation, circulation, institutional response, or another second state.

Every verso should have one unmistakable centre of gravity. Name the dominant subject assigned to each denomination and explain how supporting scenes remain subordinate.

The reverse should not become an inventory of everything associated with the motif. It should present one world, institution, event, landscape, interior, or system whose organisation reveals the theme.

Avoid explanatory devices that repeat what the scene already communicates. Arrows, recipes, stages, timelines, or strips of icons should appear only when they are conceptually indispensable.

State whether recto and verso depict the same place, related places, or conceptually linked but spatially distinct scenes. Explain the reason.

Give three project-specific questions that every verso must answer.

### 6.4 Series progression

Explain the ordering from €5 to €200 as a complete argument.

For each transition, state what the later denomination adds, changes, or reframes.

Identify one or more of the following:

a foundation;

a change of register;

a midpoint;

a culmination;

a return or cycle;

a counterpoint.

Explain why the final denomination closes the sequence. The highest value does not need to represent the "best" subject, but its role should feel conclusive within the chosen logic.

Acknowledge colour constraints where they materially affected the ordering. The proposal should show how constraints generated the narrative rather than pretending that only one assignment was possible.

## 7. Monetary design principles

### 7.1 Visual authority

Explain how the design will read as money before its theme is analysed.

Address:

large denomination numerals;

institutional scripts;

borders;

map;

flag;

signature field;

serial areas;

tactile reservations;

security-ready zones;

tonal hierarchy;

repetition across the series.

The design should not resemble an illustration placed on a coloured rectangle. Monetary elements and narrative imagery must belong to one composition.

### 7.2 Detail as evidence of value

Explain why visual density matters.

A banknote is handled repeatedly and may circulate for years. It should contain more information than can be absorbed immediately.

Identify where detail is concentrated and what it communicates. Fine information may appear in:

architecture;

natural texture;

technical systems;

clothing;

flora and fauna;

diagrams;

notation;

maps;

borders;

surface patterns;

small public activities.

The proposal should connect detail with care, continuity, and institutional capacity rather than with decoration alone.

### 7.3 Complexity without clutter

Define the hierarchy of scale.

Large forms should establish recognition.

Medium-scale scenes should explain the narrative.

Fine detail should reward inspection.

State where visual rest is preserved. Quiet areas may include sky, water, paper tone, walls, open ground, light fields, or other project-specific surfaces.

No small detail should compete with the denomination numeral or obscure compulsory institutional elements.

### 7.4 Two distances of reading

List the features read from a distance and those discovered at close range.

The distance reading should include:

denomination;

colour family;

large motif;

main silhouette;

overall light-dark distribution;

physical proportion.

The close reading should include project-specific symbols, actions, transitions, references, and cross-denomination links.

Test the series as thumbnails, in grayscale, and under partial occlusion.

### 7.5 Print language

Define the relationship between the theme's visual language and the graphic language of currency.

Specify the role of:

engraving;

linework;

stipple;

halftone;

flat colour;

translucent overprint;

paper tone;

guilloche;

vector geometry;

photographic or rendered imagery;

manual drawing;

or other techniques.

Do not apply one treatment uniformly when it suppresses the subject's identity.

State which surfaces preserve their source character and which elements receive stronger monetary treatment.

Exclude visual effects that make the note resemble a screen, advertisement, game interface, poster, or cinematic rendering unless the project explicitly justifies them.

### 7.6 Horizontal use

Explain how the design responds to the physical use of banknotes.

All principal numerals, scripts, scenes, and hierarchies should be readable in the intended orientation.

Consider:

counting;

stacking;

fanning;

wallet storage;

passing across a counter;

partial visibility;

quick verification.

Contemporary character should arise from the concept and design system rather than from imitating the dominant screen format of the period.

## 8. Colour policy

### 8.1 General rule

Define the overall saturation, contrast, and print behaviour.

The prescribed denomination colours should remain recognisable, but the series should control saturation through paper tone, neutral support colours, overprint, line density, and restrained accents.

State whether colour should appear absorbed into paper, layered above it, luminous, flat, translucent, atmospheric, or handled through another project-specific logic.

Avoid unqualified instructions such as "vibrant" or "cinematic." Describe where strong colour is allowed and how much of the visible surface it occupies.

### 8.2 Shared palette structure

Define a repeatable palette model for all denominations.

A useful four-part model is:

a near-white paper or anchor tone;

a mildly saturated denomination hue;

a dark structural tone;

a limited high-chroma accent.

Adapt the model when the theme requires a different system, but preserve equivalent hierarchy.

Explain how tonal transitions are produced. Possible methods include overprint, line density, flat fields, gradients, texture, or controlled lighting.

## 13. Continuity and stewardship

Explain how the series handles time, maintenance, responsibility, and the consequences of its subject.

This section should be adapted to the theme.

For ecological themes, it may address habitat, seasonal change, human responsibility, restoration, and resilience.

For cultural themes, it may address preservation, transmission, reinterpretation, access, and continuity between generations.

For technological themes, it may address public benefit, obsolescence, maintenance, inclusion, and material cost.

2. activation or use;
3. public consequence;
4. maintenance;
5. adaptation;
6. renewal.

Do not turn the series into a campaign unless advocacy is the explicit theme. Responsibility should arise from the subject's real history and future.
